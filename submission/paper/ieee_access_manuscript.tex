\begin{abstract}
In Internet of Things (IoT) cybersecurity, deep reconstruction-based autoencoders provide effective detection of zero-day network intrusions without requiring labeled attack samples during training. However, translating continuous reconstruction errors into operational mitigation decisions faces critical vulnerabilities: static decision thresholds explode in false alarms under non-stationary traffic surges, un-guarded adaptive thresholds suffer severe threshold inflation collapse during attack floods, defensive reinforcement learning policies risk evaluation split contamination, and existing literature relies on abstract non-standardized cryptographic assumptions. This paper presents an empirically rigorous, leakage-resistant benchmark evaluating an integrated five-stage cybersecurity framework on CICIoT2023 across 5 random seeds ($42, 123, 456, 789, 2026$). Our empirical results establish that: (1) Sample-level Dense Autoencoders ($L=1$) eliminate sequence window boundary contamination, achieving an unsupervised baseline F1-Score of $0.8854 \pm 0.0013$ ($\text{FPR} = 4.93\%$); (2) Excluded EWMA thresholding prevents inflation collapse, reaching F1 = $0.9014 \pm 0.0018$ and coming within 2.55\% of a supervised XGBoost oracle (F1 = $0.9269 \pm 0.0004$); (3) Static 95th percentile thresholds suffer a +454.4\% explosion in false positive rates under $2.0\times$ synthetic rate feature scaling; (4) Under strictly un-contaminated evaluation ($X_{\text{sup}}$ training, held-out $X_{\text{test}}$ evaluation), Deep Q-Networks achieve $71.20\%$ attack containment with $0.77\%$ false block rate, while Tabular Q-Learning achieves $0.00\%$ false block rate in isolated testing with ultra-low decision latency ($0.0757$~ms); (5) Standardized NIST FIPS 203 ML-KEM-768 key encapsulation paired with AES-256-GCM achieves sub-millisecond setup ($0.4293$~ms KEM, $0.4754$~ms setup) and 100\% tamper rejection; (6) HMAC-SHA-256 ($0.0332$~ms) and Tamper-Evident Hash-Chain Audit Logging ($0.0409$~ms append, $\mathcal{O}(1)$ scaling) provide verifiable sequence integrity and tamper localization; (7) In sensitivity testing, evaluating on a clean test set ($N=98,543$) purging all 1,457 cross-partition duplicates shifts F1 by only $-0.0009$ ($-0.10\%$), proving natural packet header repetitions do not bias results; and (8) The full integrated framework achieves an end-to-end F1-Score of $0.8968 \pm 0.0013$ and $80.65\% \pm 3.16\%$ attack containment with sub-millisecond processing latency ($0.3781 \pm 0.0225$~ms, $2,652.4 \pm 154.8$~events/s throughput).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
\PARstart{T}{he} rapid proliferation of Internet of Things (IoT) deployments in mission-critical infrastructure, industrial automated manufacturing, smart cities, and healthcare has drastically magnified the attack surface of networked systems \cite{arjunan2024}. Modern edge gateways face sophisticated, multi-vector intrusion streams characterized by massive volumetric floods (e.g., Mirai botnets, distributed denial of service, TCP SYN floods) alongside low-rate stealthy probing attacks \cite{neto2023}. Because traditional signature-based Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) rely on predefined rules that fail against novel zero-day attacks, machine learning paradigms—specifically deep unsupervised autoencoders—have emerged as a primary defensive mechanism \cite{alhawawreh2023}.

By training exclusively on normal telemetry records, autoencoders learn compact representations of benign network flow behavior. During operational inference, flows generating reconstruction errors $E_t = \|X_t - \hat{X}_t\|^2$ exceeding a calibrated threshold $\tau$ are flagged as anomalies. However, deploying continuous anomaly detectors into automated, real-time edge gateways reveals five critical methodological and architectural bottlenecks:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Threshold Sensitivity and Inflation Collapse}: Static percentile thresholds calibrated offline ($\tau_{95}$) are rigid; non-stationary traffic rate surges cause false-alarm explosions. Conversely, naive moving-average thresholds (standard EWMA) incorporate attack errors during sustained floods into background statistics, causing catastrophic \textit{threshold inflation collapse} that blinds the detector.
    \item \textbf{Evaluation Split Contamination in Defensive RL}: Automated mitigation policies driven by reinforcement learning (RL) are frequently evaluated on the same data partitions used for environment simulation, creating data leakage and masking real-world degradation.
    \item \textbf{Detector Noise Propagation}: While standalone RL policies achieve near-zero false block rates in idealized simulations, deploying them downstream of statistical anomaly detectors exposes them to upstream detector noise, causing unintended blocking of benign traffic.
    \item \textbf{Abstract Post-Quantum Assumptions}: Existing IoT security proposals frequently rely on unstandardized or theoretical post-quantum cryptographic primitives without measuring latency and payload overhead on edge-gateway hardware.
    \item \textbf{Audit Log Trust Boundaries}: Evidence logs are often loosely claimed to be ``immutable'' without formal threat modeling regarding privileged host adversaries who possess write access to local storage.
\end{enumerate}

To resolve these bottlenecks, this paper presents an empirically verified, leak-free cybersecurity framework for host-class IoT edge gateways evaluated on the CICIoT2023 benchmark \cite{neto2023}. Across eight rigorous pre-registered experiments (E1--E8) evaluated over 5 random seeds ($42, 123, 456, 789, 2026$), we deliver:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A sample-level ($L=1$) Dense Autoencoder eliminating sequence window boundary leakage.
    \item An \textbf{Excluded EWMA} adaptive thresholding algorithm that updates background statistics strictly on un-flagged flows, preventing threshold inflation collapse.
    \item A leak-free reinforcement learning defense policy (DQN and Tabular Q-Learning) trained strictly on a supervisory split ($X_{\text{sup}}$) and evaluated on held-out test data ($X_{\text{test}}$).
    \item Complete microbenchmarks of standardized \textbf{NIST FIPS 203 ML-KEM-768} post-quantum key encapsulation paired with AES-256-GCM AEAD encryption.
    \item A tamper-evident append-only hash-chain audit log with formal adversary modeling ($\mathcal{A}_{\text{proc}}$ vs. $\mathcal{A}_{\text{root}}$).
    \item An empirical 5-seed deduplication sensitivity evaluation proving that natural packet header repetitions do not inflate performance.
    \item An integrated end-to-end ablation study demonstrating real-time throughput of $2,652.4$~events/s at $0.3781$~ms latency on host edge gateways.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Base Paper Dissection (Saeed 2025)}
In a foundational work, Saeed \cite{saeed2025} proposed an AI-driven framework for IoT combining an LSTM Autoencoder, reinforcement learning response, post-quantum encryption, and salted SHA-256 evidence logging. However, rigorous inspection of Saeed (2025) reveals three foundational limitations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Evaluation Metric Absence}: The original paper evaluated an LSTM-AE with a 30-second temporal lookback on a private, unreleased 150k dataset and reported zero global test-stream metrics (presenting only five sample timestamp rows).
    \item \textit{Sequence Window Contamination}: Applying temporal sliding windows ($L > 1$) across shuffled flow records induces artificial cross-boundary contamination.
    \item \textit{Static Thresholding}: Saeed (2025) utilized static percentile thresholding and static rule escalation, which are vulnerable to non-stationary traffic rate shifts.
\end{enumerate}
In this work, we implement a rigorous \textbf{Study-Defined Baseline Adapted from Saeed (2025)} using a sample-level Dense Autoencoder on the public CICIoT2023 dataset under identical splits to provide an honest, reproducible benchmark.

\subsection{Adaptive Anomaly Thresholding}
Stream anomaly detection methods often rely on Extreme Value Theory (EVT) \cite{siffer2017} or moving-average statistics. However, high-rate IoT traffic presents rapid transitions between benign bursts and attack floods. When an un-guarded adaptive threshold updates its mean and variance during attack bursts, the threshold inflates alongside the attack errors, blinding the system. Developing thresholding mechanisms that distinguish non-stationary benign surges from malicious volumetric floods is an active research challenge.

\subsection{Post-Quantum Cryptography Standardization}
In August 2024, the National Institute of Standards and Technology released FIPS 203 \cite{nist2024fips203}, standardizing Module-Lattice-Based Key-Encapsulation Mechanisms (ML-KEM). While post-quantum cryptography is mathematically mature, measuring its computational latency, ciphertext overhead, and memory footprint inside real-time IoT gateway processing pipelines is essential for practical adoption.

\section{Research Gap and Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}
While individual components of anomaly detection, reinforcement learning, cryptography, and logging have been investigated in silos, existing literature suffers from: (1) subtle cross-partition data leakage; (2) unmeasured threshold inflation collapse; and (3) idealized RL simulations that ignore upstream detector noise.

This study provides five concrete, testable contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Algorithmic Solution to Threshold Inflation}: We design and validate \textit{Excluded EWMA}, which updates background statistics strictly on un-flagged flow events, preventing inflation collapse during volumetric attacks while maintaining high detection recall ($84.01\%$).
    \item \textbf{Un-Contaminated Defensive Policy Formulation}: We establish a leak-free evaluation protocol separating supervisory policy training ($X_{\text{sup}}$) from held-out evaluation ($X_{\text{test}}$), demonstrating that Deep Q-Networks achieve $71.20\%$ containment with $0.77\%$ false block rate.
    \item \textbf{Quantification of Detector Noise Propagation}: We identify and reconcile the performance delta between standalone RL evaluation ($0.00\%$ false block rate) and integrated streaming ($7.39\%$ false block rate), proving that upstream detector false alarms amplify downstream mitigation disruption.
    \item \textbf{Edge Validation of Standardized NIST FIPS 203}: We implement and microbenchmark concrete ML-KEM-768 key encapsulation with AES-256-GCM AEAD, proving sub-millisecond edge viability ($0.4293$~ms KEM, $0.4754$~ms setup) and 100\% tamper rejection.
    \item \textbf{Deduplication Sensitivity Verification}: We execute an empirical 5-seed evaluation on a clean deduplicated test set ($N=98,543$), proving that natural feature repetitions across partitions shift F1-scores by less than $0.1\%$.
\end{enumerate}

\section{System Architecture and Security Threat Model}
\label{sec:architecture}

\subsection{System Architecture}
The framework operates as a synchronous five-stage pipeline deployed on industrial host-class IoT edge gateways (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stage 1 (Ingestion \& Normalization)}: Extracts 39 numerical features per flow record ($L=1$). Drops identity metadata (`src\_ip`, `dst\_ip`, `port`, `Timestamp`). Normalizes features using `StandardScaler` parameters calibrated strictly on $X_{\text{train}}$.
    \item \textbf{Stage 2 (Detection \& Threshold Adaptation)}: Computes Dense-AE reconstruction loss $E_t = \frac{1}{D}\sum_{d=1}^D (x_{t,d} - \hat{x}_{t,d})^2$. Evaluates against dynamic threshold $\tau_t$ via Excluded EWMA.
    \item \textbf{Stage 3 (Automated Mitigation)}: Maintains a consecutive alert streak counter $S_t$. An RL agent (DQN or Tabular Q) maps state $(E_t, \Delta E_t, S_t)$ to defensive actions: Allow (0), Rate-Limit (1), Deep Packet Inspection (2), or Block (3).
    \item \textbf{Stage 4 (Cryptographic Encapsulation)}: Encapsulates mitigation command records using NIST FIPS 203 ML-KEM-768, deriving symmetric session keys via HKDF-SHA256 to encrypt transit telemetry using AES-256-GCM AEAD.
    \item \textbf{Stage 5 (Tamper-Evident Forensic Logging)}: Appends event records into a cryptographic hash chain, maintaining verifiable sequential order and localizing unauthorized tampering.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Security Threat Model \& Trust Boundaries}
We formally state the adversary models and trust boundaries:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Network Transit Adversary ($\mathcal{A}_{\text{net}}$)}: Possesses full man-in-the-middle capabilities on network transit links between IoT gateways and cloud aggregation nodes. $\mathcal{A}_{\text{net}}$ can eavesdrop, inject, modify, replay, and tamper with transit records. Standardized ML-KEM-768 key encapsulation and AES-256-GCM AEAD guarantee ciphertext confidentiality, authenticity, and 100\% tamper rejection.
    \item \textbf{Local Unprivileged Process Adversary ($\mathcal{A}_{\text{proc}}$)}: Operates on the host gateway without administrative root privileges. $\mathcal{A}_{\text{proc}}$ attempts to modify, delete, reorder, or inject audit log entries. The append-only hash chain guarantees sequence integrity: modifying any record $r_i$ invalidates hash $h_i = \text{SHA-256}(h_{i-1} \,\|\, r_i \,\|\, \text{seq}_i)$, instantly detected during verification.
    \item \textbf{Privileged Local Host Adversary ($\mathcal{A}_{\text{root}}$)}: Possesses administrative write access to local storage. An $\mathcal{A}_{\text{root}}$ adversary can rewrite the \textit{entire} local chain from the genesis block forward, recomputing all valid hashes:
    \begin{equation}
    h'_k = \text{SHA-256}(h'_{k-1} \,\|\, r'_k \,\|\, \text{seq}_k) \quad \forall k \ge i
    \end{equation}
    \textit{Explicit Boundary Disclosure}: A local cryptographic hash chain alone does not provide distributed Byzantine consensus. To achieve complete anti-rewriting immutability against $\mathcal{A}_{\text{root}}$, the latest tip hash ($h_N$) must be periodically anchored to an external trusted timestamping authority or distributed ledger.
\end{itemize}

\section{Dataset and Experimental Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Dataset Provenance & Preprocessing}
We evaluate our protocol on \textbf{CICIoT2023} \cite{neto2023}, a modern benchmark capturing 33 IoT attack types alongside legitimate traffic across 105 topology devices. Non-numeric metadata identifiers (`src\_ip`, `dst\_ip`, `src\_port`, `dst\_port`, `Timestamp`, `flow\_id`) are explicitly dropped to eliminate shortcut memorization. Models operate on 39 numerical statistical flow features at sample-level granularity ($L=1$).

\subsection{Leak-Free Partitioning Protocol}
To ensure strict split isolation, data is partitioned as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Benign Training Split ($X_{\text{train}}$)}: 100,000 pure benign flow records used exclusively for Autoencoder training and `StandardScaler` parameter fitting ($n_{\text{samples\_seen}} = 100,000$).
    \item \textbf{Benign Validation Split ($X_{\text{val}}$)}: 30,000 pure benign flows reserved for static percentile calibration ($\tau_{95}, \tau_{99}$) and EWMA initialization.
    \item \textbf{Supervisory Partition ($X_{\text{sup}}$)}: 40,000 50/50 balanced benign and attack flows reserved for supervised reference training and reinforcement learning policy training.
    \item \textbf{Held-Out Evaluation Split ($X_{\text{test}}$)}: 100,000 flows (14,911 benign, 85,089 attack) strictly reserved for held-out evaluation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Cross-Partition Duplicate Overlap & Deduplicated Sensitivity Protocol}
High-rate network flooding naturally produces repeated packet headers across volumetric bursts (e.g., uniform 40-byte TCP SYN packets). In our partitioned dataset, 81 unique feature patterns (215 rows, 0.215\%) in $X_{\text{test}}$ overlap with $X_{\text{train}}$, and 953 unique patterns (1,402 rows, 1.402\%) overlap with $X_{\text{sup}}$ (total overlapping rows: 1,457, or 1.457\%).

To evaluate whether duplicate feature vectors artificially bias model performance, we constructed a \textbf{Clean Deduplicated Test Set} ($N=98,543$) by purging all 1,457 overlapping rows (60 benign, 1,397 attack). We re-evaluated all models across all 5 random seeds to measure the sensitivity shift ($\Delta = \text{Clean} - \text{Full}$).

\subsection{Deployment Hardware Boundaries}
All microbenchmarks were executed on an AMD64 host architecture (Windows 11 x86\_64, 3.40~GHz host CPU, 16~GB RAM), representative of host-class industrial IoT edge gateways, fog aggregation nodes, and smart hub servers (e.g., Raspberry Pi 4/5, Intel Atom/NUC industrial gateways). Deeply embedded microcontrollers (<100~MHz clock, <64~KB RAM) operate under strict cycle budgets; executing deep neural networks and lattice-based ML-KEM-768 on microcontrollers requires dedicated integer-only quantization (TFLite Micro) and assembly optimization, which represents a separate research trajectory.

\section{Proposed Methods and Algorithms}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Sample-Level Dense Autoencoder}
The anomaly detector utilizes a symmetric feedforward architecture with layer dimensions $39 \to 32 \to 16 \to 32 \to 39$, utilizing ReLU activations and batch normalization. The model is trained using Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}} = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{b=1}^B \frac{1}{D} \sum_{d=1}^D (x_{b,d} - \hat{x}_{b,d})^2
\end{equation}
Sample-level inference ($L=1$) processes each flow independently, eliminating sequence window boundary leakage.

\subsection{Excluded EWMA Adaptive Thresholding}
To overcome threshold inflation collapse, we propose \textbf{Excluded EWMA}. Let $E_t$ denote the reconstruction error at step $t$. Background statistics $(\mu_t, \sigma_t^2)$ are updated with smoothing parameter $\alpha \in (0, 1)$ only when $E_t$ falls below the current threshold $\tau_t$:
\begin{equation}
\tau_t = \mu_t + k \cdot \sigma_t
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\hat{y}_t = \mathbb{I}(E_t > \tau_t)
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mu_{t+1} = 
\begin{cases} 
(1 - \alpha)\mu_t + \alpha E_t & \text{if } \hat{y}_t = 0 \\
\mu_t & \text{if } \hat{y}_t = 1
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{t+1}^2 = 
\begin{cases} 
(1 - \alpha)\sigma_t^2 + \alpha (E_t - \mu_t)^2 & \text{if } \hat{y}_t = 0 \\
\sigma_t^2 & \text{if } \hat{y}_t = 1
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Because error statistics update strictly on non-flagged events ($\hat{y}_t = 0$), attack floods cannot inflate the threshold.

\subsection{Un-Contaminated Defensive Policy Formulation}
We formulate automated defense as a Markov Decision Process (MDP):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{State Space}: $s_t = (E_t, \Delta E_t, S_t)$, where $E_t$ is normalized reconstruction error, $\Delta E_t = E_t - E_{t-1}$, and $S_t$ is the consecutive alert streak counter.
    \item \textbf{Action Space}: $a_t \in \{0: \text{Allow}, 1: \text{Rate-Limit}, 2: \text{DPI}, 3: \text{Block}\}$.
    \item \textbf{Reward Function}:
    \begin{equation}
    R(s, a, y) = 
    \begin{cases}
    +10 \cdot a - 2 & \text{if } y = 1 \text{ (Attack Mitigation)} \\
    -15 \cdot a & \text{if } y = 0 \text{ (Disruption Penalty)}
    \end{cases}
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}
We evaluate Tabular Q-Learning (state discretization into 1,000 bins) and Deep Q-Networks (DQN, 2-layer MLP with experience replay and target network) \cite{mnih2015}.

\subsection{Standardized NIST FIPS 203 ML-KEM Workflow}
Post-quantum transport security utilizes ML-KEM-768 \cite{nist2024fips203}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Gateway generates keypair $(pk, sk) \leftarrow \text{ML-KEM.KeyGen}()$.
    \item Aggregator encapsulates shared secret $(ct, ss) \leftarrow \text{ML-KEM.Encaps}(pk)$.
    \item Gateway decapsulates $ss \leftarrow \text{ML-KEM.Decaps}(sk, ct)$.
    \item Symmetric key derived via HKDF: $K_{\text{enc}} = \text{HKDF-SHA256}(ss, \text{salt}, \text{info})$.
    \item Mitigation payload encrypted via AES-256-GCM AEAD: $C, T \leftarrow \text{AES-GCM.Encrypt}(K_{\text{enc}}, IV, P, AAD)$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Tamper-Evident Hash Chain Audit Log}
Evidence records $r_i$ (containing timestamp, flow features, detection score, mitigation action, and ciphertext) are sequenced via cryptographic chaining \cite{haber1991,bellare1996}:
\begin{equation}
h_i = \text{SHA-256}(h_{i-1} \,\|\, r_i \,\|\, \text{seq}_i)
\end{equation}
Verification iterates through $i = 1, \dots, N$. Any altered block $j$ violates $h_j = \text{SHA-256}(h_{j-1} \,\|\, r'_j \,\|\, \text{seq}_j)$, identifying the exact point of tampering.

\subsection{Multi-Dimensional Ablation Orthogonality}
A critical observation in Table~\ref{tab:e7_ablation} is that removing Post-Quantum Cryptography or Hash Chain Logging leaves the detection F1-score exactly unchanged ($0.8968 \pm 0.0013$). This invariance is expected due to the pipelined architecture: anomaly detection executes first, mapping telemetry features to reconstruction errors and binary decisions. Cryptographic encapsulation and append-only audit logging operate downstream on the resulting event record. Consequently, ablating cryptographic modules removes transport security and verifiable audit properties without modifying upstream feature loss or detection boundaries.

\subsection{Reconciliation of False Block Rates}
In standalone policy evaluation (Experiment E4), Tabular Q-Learning achieved a $0.00\%$ false block rate under idealized conditions. In contrast, in the integrated pipeline (Table~\ref{tab:e7_ablation}), the false block rate is $7.39\% \pm 2.51\%$. This difference reflects upstream detector noise propagation: Excluded EWMA thresholding has an empirical false positive rate of $\approx 13.6\%$ on bursty benign traffic. These false alarms increment the consecutive alert streak counter, causing the downstream Q-policy to escalate mitigation on subsequent benign flows. This realistic behavior highlights the necessity of end-to-end integrated evaluation over isolated micro-benchmarks.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
Our findings demonstrate that real-time IoT gateway cybersecurity requires balancing detection sensitivity against operational disruption. While Excluded EWMA boosts detection recall by $+3.88\%$ and prevents inflation collapse during floods, it exhibits an empirical $13.6\%$ false positive rate on bursty benign flows. In isolation, automated response policies achieve $0.00\%$ false block rates; however, in integrated pipelines, upstream false alarms propagate into downstream mitigation, causing a $7.39\%$ false block rate. This underscores the necessity of evaluating integrated multi-stage pipelines rather than isolated microbenchmarks.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limitations}
We openly disclose four material limitations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Pre-Shuffled Dataset Origin}: The public CICIoT2023 dataset was shuffled across captures by the original authors, preventing continuous day-by-day PCAP session reconstruction. Our duplicate-exclusion sensitivity check proves that repeated feature patterns do not bias evaluation metrics, but independent physical testbeds are required to prove zero-day cross-device generalization.
    \item \textbf{Local Storage Trust Boundary}: The append-only hash chain guarantees sequence integrity against unprivileged processes ($\mathcal{A}_{\text{proc}}$), but cannot prevent a root host adversary ($\mathcal{A}_{\text{root}}$) from rewriting local storage without external tip anchoring.
    \item \textbf{Hardware Deployment Scope}: Latency and throughput were measured on an AMD64 host edge gateway (3.40~GHz). Constrained microcontrollers (<100~MHz) require dedicated integer quantization and assembly optimization.
    \item \textbf{Synthetic Feature Scaling}: Experiment E8 applied analytical scaling to benign rate features rather than live network packet arrivals.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This study presented a leak-free empirical evaluation of adaptive anomaly thresholding, un-contaminated reinforcement learning defense, standardized post-quantum cryptography, and cryptographic audit logging on CICIoT2023. Excluded EWMA resolves threshold inflation collapse (F1 = $0.9014 \pm 0.0018$), DQN achieves $71.20\%$ attack containment with $0.77\%$ false block rate, ML-KEM-768 provides sub-millisecond post-quantum transport security ($0.4293$~ms), and the full integrated framework achieves an end-to-end F1-Score of $0.8968 \pm 0.0013$ and $80.65\% \pm 3.16\%$ containment with sub-millisecond processing latency ($0.3781$~ms per event, $2,652.4$~events/s throughput).
